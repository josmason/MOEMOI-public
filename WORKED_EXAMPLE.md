# WORKED EXAMPLE: Running the Intelligence Process on Critical Feedback

> **TLDR**
> **WHAT:** A step-by-step walkthrough of one full Intelligence Process (IP) cycle — all four phases, named practices labelled, using a realistic scenario.
> **WHY:** The IP can be described abstractly; this example shows what it feels like and what to actually do, substep by substep.
> **HOW IT AFFECTS YOU:** Read this once before starting your first full IP run. Return to it when a specific substep feels unclear.

---

**Scenario:** You receive unexpected critical feedback from a respected colleague on work you have invested three months in. They argue your approach has a structural flaw and you should reconsider the core direction. Your immediate reaction is defensiveness.

---

## Phase 1 — Experience
*(Substeps: Sense, Attend, Perceive, Feel, Desire)*

**Sense.** You read the message. The words register.

**Attend.** You notice your attention narrowing — you are re-reading the harshest sentence repeatedly and skipping the specifics. This is attention-hijack: working memory has locked onto the threat signal rather than the content.

*Pause. Broaden.*

**Perceive.** Read the whole message once, slowly. Note what is actually there: they have identified two specific weaknesses (not a blanket rejection), they have offered an alternative direction, and they said the underlying goal is sound. The gap is the structural approach, not the purpose.

**Feel.** *Affect-labelling practice:* Name what you are feeling before proceeding. "Stung. Defensive. Some uncertainty about whether they are right." Naming it reduces reactivity and clarifies what you are processing — this is a threat response, not a logical conclusion.

**Desire.** What do you actually want here? To be right, or to produce the best outcome? The void: a gap between your current approach and the most optimal one. The feedback is a signal that the gap may be larger than you knew.

---

## Phase 2 — Understand
*(Substeps: Question, Retrieve, Store, Define, Transform, Simulate, Evaluate, Prioritise, Decide)*

**Question (MEQ step — Most Empowering Question).** The literal question is "Is this person wrong?" The most empowering question is: "What would have to be true for this feedback to be correct, and what evidence would I look for?"

**Retrieve (R1: Priming Scan).** Before reading their alternative proposal, spend two minutes recalling your own model: what assumptions underlie your current approach? What did you take for granted? Write them down. Now compare against the feedback. Which of your assumptions does their critique directly challenge?

**Store.** Capture the specific objections verbatim. Do not paraphrase yet — paraphrasing introduces your own framing before you have processed the raw signal.

**Define.** What is the actual gap? Not "they criticised my work" — that is a self-referential frame. The real gap: "the structural architecture may not achieve the stated goal as efficiently as an alternative could." This is a solvable, testable question.

**Transform (T1: Connection Statement).** Write one sentence: "This feedback confirms / contradicts / extends what I believed about [X]." If it contradicts: what belief, specifically, needs to be revised? Forced articulation surfaces whether you genuinely disagree or are defending the work because it is yours.

**U3: Causal Account.** What is driving the situation? (1) Observed state: your approach has a structural flaw per a credible critique. (2) Evidence: two specific weaknesses identified, with an alternative that addresses both. (3) Most likely distortion: the sunk-cost effect — three months of investment makes the critique feel larger than it is. What would have to be true for this account to be wrong? The colleague would need to have misunderstood your constraint set. That is testable: ask one clarifying question.

**Simulate (SIM1: WOOP Pre-Mortem).** Run two scenarios:

- *Scenario A — Apply the feedback:* rework the structural approach. Outcome: 2-3 weeks of revision, potentially stronger final product. Obstacle: ego investment making the rework feel like failure. Plan: rename it as "v2 refinement" and treat it as a normal iteration.
- *Scenario B — Defend the current approach:* respond with a counter-argument. Outcome: colleague either accepts or the disagreement stalls. Pre-mortem: what is the most likely cause of failure? If they are right and you defended anyway, you ship a structurally weaker product. This is the higher-cost error.

**Evaluate.** Which scenario has the highest expected value? Scenario A, unless the clarifying question reveals the critique rests on a misunderstanding.

**Prioritise (P1: Contamination Check).** Is your current ordering (defend first) driven by genuine return on investment or by discomfort avoidance? Avoidance-inversion check: the high-importance action (honestly evaluating the critique) is currently ranked below the comfortable action (writing a counter-argument). That is a contaminated priority. Correct it.

**Decide.** Commit to Scenario A, conditional on the clarifying question. The decision is not "they are right" — it is "I will investigate with an honest prior rather than a defensive one."

---

## Phase 3 — Prepare
*(Substeps: Resource, Become Capable, Schedule, Ensure-Execution; heart of this phase is Define)*

**Define (north-star check).** Your north star is the most optimal outcome for the project. "Being right about the approach" is not the north star. Realigning.

**Schedule.** Block two hours tomorrow for the structural review.

**PR3: Committed Action.** "When I sit down at 9am, the first thing I do is re-read their alternative proposal, not my defence." Specify the discomfort you expect: it will feel like conceding. Name it now so it does not stop you tomorrow.

---

## Phase 4 — Optimise
*(Substeps: Do, Re-Experience/Archive)*

**Do.** Run the structural review with an honest prior. You find: one of their two objections is valid and significant; the other rests on a constraint they did not know about. You write back, acknowledging the valid objection and explaining the constraint behind the other.

**Re-Experience.** Was the gap closed? The valid objection: addressed — structural revision in progress. The misunderstood constraint: now shared with the colleague, who has updated their view. The conversation has become collaborative rather than adversarial.

**Re-Evaluate / Archive.** What changed in your model?

- You carry a sunk-cost blind spot in high-investment work — now flagged as a recurring Perceive risk.
- Critical feedback from credible sources is more reliably useful when processed through the IP than when processed through a first-response reflex.

The gap is smaller. The model is better. The loop continues.
